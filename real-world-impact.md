# From Prototype to Real-World Impact

## 1. Purpose and who takes part

This is the **common mandatory challenge for all 15 Grand Finale teams** at
the Zetech Forge Alumni & Student Hackathon 2026. The top three teams from each
of the five tracks qualify.

**It is assessed only during Stage 2: Grand Finale Pitch & Awards Judging.
It is not part of Track-Level Judging.** Only qualifying finalists are
required to prepare and deliver this presentation.

Take the technical solution, prototype or application developed for your
selected hackathon challenge and show how it could address a **similar or
closely related real-world problem**. Explain how it could be adapted,
implemented and scaled in that environment. You are **not expected to develop
an unrelated new project or deploy a production system during the hackathon**.

Make this chain clear:

1. The original challenge your team completed.
2. The technical solution or outcome you developed.
3. The related real-world problem you identified.
4. How your existing components could address that problem.
5. What adaptation, resources and validation real-world implementation needs.

The challenge assesses technical understanding, creativity, problem-solving,
implementation thinking and awareness of societal impact. It is **not simply
a business pitch or a repeat of your original challenge demonstration**.

## 2. What finalists are expected to explain

Use the following preparation areas to build one coherent application
proposal. They expand the assessment themes; they are not separate point
pools. The [seven scoring criteria](#5-grand-finale-assessment-30-points)
remain the basis of the 30-point assessment.

### A. Real-world problem identification and context

Identify a specific problem, not only a broad ambition such as "improve
healthcare." Explain:

- **Problem and setting:** what happens, where it occurs and which sector it
  belongs to.
- **Affected people:** who experiences the problem, who is excluded or
  underserved, and why the consequences matter.
- **Evidence:** credible sources, available statistics, documented cases or
  observations supporting the problem. Cite their source, date and context;
  distinguish observations and assumptions from established facts.
- **Existing approaches:** how people currently manage the problem, which
  solutions already exist and what limitations or gaps remain.

Relevant sectors include healthcare, agriculture, education, financial
services, manufacturing, transportation and logistics, environmental
conservation, public safety, governance and public services, and energy and
utilities. Choose the setting your solution can credibly serve; do not claim
every sector as a target.

### B. Connection to the selected hackathon challenge

Name your track and original challenge, then describe what your team actually
built. Identify the functions, rules, model, contract, workflow, hardware or
other components you would reuse.

Explain what stays the same, what must be adapted or extended, and why those
components fit the new setting. A short mapping from **original requirement
to implemented component to proposed use** can make the relationship clear.
Identify any gaps honestly. An unrelated idea with the same project name is
not a credible continuation of your hackathon outcome.

### C. Proposed solution and value proposition

Explain what your proposed solution does, who would use it and how users
would interact with it. Show how it addresses the identified gap and what
benefits users or beneficiaries could receive.

Compare it with current approaches: what is useful, different or better
suited to this context? Innovation may be a thoughtful adaptation or improved
workflow; unsupported claims that no alternatives exist are not evidence.
Clearly label **implemented functionality**, **proposed adaptations** and
**future features**.

### D. How the solution works

Walk through the main sequence:

1. Where inputs or information come from.
2. Which components receive and process them.
3. How decisions, calculations or rule matches occur.
4. What outputs are generated.
5. How a user acts on those outputs and benefits from them.

Demonstrate the relevant working prototype, application, simulation, model,
detection system or other technical output from the hackathon. Use a live
demonstration, system walkthrough, workflow diagram, screenshots or a short
recording as appropriate. Show what actually works; a design diagram alone
does not establish that its proposed components have been implemented.

### E. Technical architecture and technology stack

Use a simple architecture diagram to explain:

- Languages, frameworks and the technology stack used during the hackathon.
- Hardware and software components, where applicable.
- Databases, data sources, APIs and integrations.
- How components communicate, where processing happens and how data flows
  from input to output.
- Security and privacy boundaries, access controls and sensitive-data
  handling.
- Additional infrastructure or technologies needed for the real-world
  application.

Justify important choices against the needs and constraints of the proposed
setting. Mark new or planned components separately from those demonstrated
in the prototype. Listing technology names without explaining their roles
does not establish implementation understanding.

### F. Real-world implementation and deployment plan

Describe where the solution would run and how it would be introduced into
the intended environment. Cover required infrastructure, hardware, software,
data access, system integrations, resources and expertise.

A realistic roadmap might be:

1. **Refine and validate:** close known technical gaps and verify safety,
   reliability and suitability for the setting.
2. **Prepare a limited pilot:** obtain permissions, arrange infrastructure,
   establish data safeguards and agree responsibilities with a host.
3. **Test and gather feedback:** evaluate operation and usability with
   authorized users, using stated success indicators.
4. **Improve:** address evidence from testing and user feedback.
5. **Expand carefully:** extend only after checking results, capacity and
   operational readiness.

Identify potential implementation partners or stakeholders, who would
operate and maintain the system, and any training or support needed. Give
reasonable cost estimates or ranges, their assumptions and the main setup
and ongoing cost drivers. **Exact commercial quotations are not required.**
Do not present hoped-for permissions or partnerships as confirmed.

### G. Feasibility, sustainability and scalability

Explain whether the plan is technically achievable and affordable to
implement and maintain. Consider:

- Required skills, resources, operational ownership and ongoing funding.
- Maintenance, updates, support, connectivity and hardware replacement where
  relevant.
- How additional users, data, devices, locations or institutions would
  affect capacity, cost and reliability.
- Adoption constraints and how the solution would remain useful over time.

A relevant business model, institutional ownership arrangement or partnership
may help explain sustainability, but a commercial model is not mandatory for
every application. Support scaling claims with assumptions and constraints;
"move to the cloud" alone is not a scaling plan.

### H. Challenges, risks and mitigation

Identify risks specific to your application and explain a practical safeguard
or response for each major risk. Consider relevant:

- Technical limitations, reliability and infrastructure or connectivity gaps.
- Financial constraints, hardware availability and maintenance.
- Cybersecurity threats, privacy, data protection and misuse.
- Ethical, legal, regulatory and physical-safety concerns.
- User adoption, accessibility and change management.
- Environmental costs, resource use and disposal.

Explain what the safeguard reduces and what uncertainty remains. Do not
claim that a prototype is secure, legally compliant or production-ready
without the necessary evidence and review.

### I. Sustainable Development Goals and societal impact

Identify the relevant **United Nations Sustainable Development Goals
(SDGs)** and, where possible, specific targets. Explain the link between
your proposed application and those goals rather than only displaying SDG
numbers or logos.

Name intended beneficiaries and the societal, economic or environmental
benefits they could experience. Explain inclusion and accessibility: who
might struggle to access or use the solution, and how would you address that?
Consider unintended harm or unequal outcomes as well as benefits.

Distinguish a plausible contribution from demonstrated impact. A successful
hackathon demonstration does not prove that the proposed societal benefits
have already occurred.

### J. Evaluation and success indicators

Choose realistic indicators that match the problem and intended outcomes.
Examples include people or organizations benefiting, time saved, operational
cost reductions, accuracy or efficiency improvements, reduced waste,
improved service delivery or accessibility, and reduced detection time or
security incidents where applicable.

For each main indicator, explain:

- The baseline or how you would establish it.
- The target or expected improvement and its assumptions.
- The measurement method and relevant evaluation period.
- The data or evidence required and how it could be collected responsibly.

Separate **measured prototype results** from **projected pilot or deployment
outcomes**. If no real-world baseline has been collected, say how you would
obtain it rather than inventing a result. For example, a lab detection-time
measurement is not evidence that an organization's incident rate has fallen.

### K. Future development and opportunities

Close with prioritized next steps: technical improvements, additional
features, further testing, pilot opportunities, research or potential
partnerships. Commercialization or institutional adoption may be relevant.

Identify the most important assumption to validate next and what evidence
would justify continuing or scaling. Keep this roadmap connected to your
existing solution rather than introducing a second project.

## 3. Presentation and demonstration requirements

| Requirement | What it means |
|---|---|
| Who presents | All 15 qualifying finalist teams present to the Grand Finale judging panel. |
| Duration | **7 minutes per team**, including the solution explanation and any demonstration. |
| Slide count | **8-10 slides; maximum 10. The title slide counts toward the limit.** |
| Questions | Any separate question-and-answer time will be communicated by the organizing committee. Do not assume it extends the seven-minute presentation. |
| Stage | These requirements apply only to the Grand Finale, not Track-Level Judging. |

Follow the announced finalist preparation, deck-submission and running-order
instructions. The code freeze is not a universal deck-submission deadline
or permission to change frozen code.

**Schedule note:** 15 presentations of seven minutes require **105 minutes
for presentations alone**, before transitions, separate questions, technical
setup, demonstration delays or Finale deliberation. The current
[Schedule](schedule.md) is a draft. The organizing committee must approve an
expanded allocation or a different approved presentation arrangement before
publishing the final timetable; follow its confirmed running order rather
than assuming the draft provides that additional time.

### Make the demonstration useful and reliable

- Choose a concise workflow that shows the component supporting your proposed
  application. Rehearse the explanation and demonstration together within
  seven minutes.
- Explain the input, expected result and observed output. Identify whether
  the evidence is live, recorded, simulated or a previous test result.
- Keep diagrams, screenshots or a short backup recording available in case
  connectivity, hardware or the approved environment is unavailable. Explain
  what the fallback shows and any functionality it cannot verify.
- Hide credentials, personal information, private infrastructure details and
  unauthorized records. Use approved test data and environments.
- Do not perform unsafe or unauthorized actions to make the demonstration
  more dramatic. A proposed real-world use does not authorize live deployment
  or testing against real users or systems.

## 4. Recommended presentation structure

The following ten-slide structure covers the preparation areas above. You
may combine related sections into **eight or nine slides**, provided all
major assessment areas remain covered. This is a structure recommendation,
not ten additional scoring criteria.

| Slide | Title | What to include |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Project Introduction and Executive Summary | Project name, track, selected challenge, team name and members, and a brief summary of the proposed real-world application. |
| 2 | Real-World Problem and Context | Specific problem, sector, setting, importance and supporting evidence. |
| 3 | Target Users and Existing Gaps | Users and beneficiaries, who experiences the problem, existing approaches and their limitations. |
| 4 | Proposed Solution and Value Proposition | What you propose, how it helps, the connection to your original challenge and what makes the approach useful or innovative. |
| 5 | How the Solution Works and Prototype Demonstration | Main workflow, functional components and a concise demonstration of the hackathon outcome. Label implemented and proposed functionality. |
| 6 | Technical Architecture and Technology Stack | Architecture diagram, languages, frameworks, hardware/software, APIs, databases, integrations, data flows and component interactions. |
| 7 | Real-World Implementation and Deployment Plan | Intended deployment environment, infrastructure, implementation phases, additional resources and integrations. |
| 8 | Feasibility, Resources and Scalability | Estimated costs and assumptions, operations, maintenance, expertise, stakeholders, partnerships, growth constraints and sustainability. |
| 9 | SDGs and Societal Impact | Relevant goals and targets, beneficiaries, societal/economic/environmental benefits, inclusion, accessibility and expected measurable outcomes. |
| 10 | Risks, Success Indicators and Next Steps | Main risks and safeguards, measurement methods and indicators, and prioritized development and validation work. |

Use diagrams, architecture illustrations and visual evidence with concise
explanations. Avoid overcrowded text or reading a long feature list. Cite
important problem evidence and label estimates clearly.

## 5. Grand Finale assessment: 30 points

The common assessment evaluates the quality of your proposed real-world
application and how credibly you translate the hackathon outcome into
practical value. **The approved criteria and maximum remain unchanged.**

| Criterion | Maximum points | Evidence your team should demonstrate |
|---|---:|---|
| Real-World Problem Identification and Sector Relevance | 5 | A specific, significant problem; its sector and setting; affected users; credible supporting evidence and gaps in current approaches. |
| Solution Relevance, Innovation and Suitability | 5 | A clear connection to your original challenge and implemented components; a suitable adaptation; a useful, differentiated value proposition. |
| Technical Architecture and Technology Stack | 5 | An understandable design and data flow; justified technologies; component interactions; a technically accurate account of what works and what remains planned. |
| Real-World Implementation Feasibility and Scalability | 5 | A realistic deployment roadmap, resources and cost assumptions, integrations, operational ownership, maintenance and growth considerations. |
| SDG Alignment and Societal Impact | 4 | Relevant SDGs and targets where possible, a reasoned link to intended benefits, named beneficiaries, inclusion and sustainability. |
| Risk Identification, Safety and Sustainability Considerations | 4 | Relevant security, privacy, technical, ethical, operational, safety and environmental risks, with credible mitigation and remaining limitations. |
| Measurable Outcomes and Evaluation Indicators | 2 | Suitable indicators, baseline/target or a plan to establish them, measurement methods and a clear separation of measured results from projections. |
| **Total** | **30** | **Assessment of the proposed real-world application, not an additional technical-stage rubric.** |

For every criterion:

- **Full points:** clear, specific and credible evidence covers the criterion.
- **Partial points:** the criterion is addressed, but evidence is incomplete,
  weakly supported or insufficiently detailed.
- **Zero points:** the criterion is not adequately addressed or its claims
  are unsupported.

The panel evaluates substance, technical understanding and feasibility,
**not presentation design alone**. An attractive deck cannot replace
evidence. There is no separate presentation-points category added to this
rubric.

## 6. Combined score and normalization

Your official **Track-Level technical score out of 100 is carried forward
unchanged**. It is the independent judges' average confirmed during Stage 1;
it is not replaced or reduced because you reach the Finale. The mandatory
challenge adds up to **30 points**, for a maximum combined score of **130**.

```text
Combined Score = Track-Level Technical Score + Mandatory Challenge Score
Maximum Combined Score = 100 + 30 = 130
Normalized Final Score = (Combined Score / 130) * 100
```

### Worked example

| Score component | Example |
|---|---:|
| Official Track-Level technical score | 88/100 |
| Grand Finale mandatory challenge score | 25/30 |
| Combined score | 113/130 |
| Normalized final score | (113 / 130) * 100 = **86.92%** |

The normalized final score ranks the **15 finalists for Overall Hackathon
Winner**. Special awards, including **Most Innovative Solution**, **Best
Social Impact Solution** and **Best Pitch & Presentation**, are selected
separately from the normalized overall ranking during the Grand Finale under
the event's published
[award arrangements](prizes.md). Special awards do not add points to the
130-point overall maximum or create unpublished scoring categories.

## 7. Track-specific technical and safety requirements

Continue following your selected track's rules. The real-world application
proposal does not relax them or authorize new targets, deployments or data
access.

| Track | Continue to observe |
|---|---|
| Software Development | Application security, privacy, safe handling of credentials and responsible development practices. |
| Machine Learning & Data Science | Data privacy, permitted data use, responsible AI, evaluation limitations and honest reporting of model performance. |
| Cybersecurity | Only designated or explicitly authorized lab systems; approved defensive tests, sanitized evidence and required cleanup/restoration. No unauthorized external-target testing. |
| Web3 & Blockchain | Designated practice-chain and wallet restrictions. Local practice accounts are not production credentials; do not use real assets or production wallet keys for the demonstration. |
| IoT & Robotics | Hardware and electrical safety, approved equipment and power arrangements, and safe operation or simulation of devices. |

A simulation, detection rule, trained model or other permitted technical
outcome can form the foundation of the proposal. Explain its limits and the
additional validation needed; do not imply that every track must produce
the same type of application or a production-ready system.

## 8. Important clarifications and preparation checklist

**No real-world deployment is required during the hackathon.** The task is
to demonstrate your existing technical outcome and explain a credible,
responsible path to implementation.

- Use the original hackathon prototype or technical outcome as the foundation.
  The new problem must be related to the challenge you completed.
- Present future functionality as a plan, not an implemented feature.
- Present projected benefits as expectations, not verified societal impact.
- State reasonable assumptions behind estimated costs, resources, deployment
  needs and outcomes. Explain what remains to be validated.
- A relevant business case can support feasibility, but this is not only a
  commercial pitch.
- The presentation and 30 additional points apply only to Stage 2. They
  do not change any track's 100-point technical assessment.

Before presenting:

1. Check that the problem-to-challenge-to-solution connection is clear.
2. Prepare 8-10 slides, counting the title slide, and rehearse within seven
   minutes including the demonstration.
3. Check that your architecture, demonstration and implementation-status
   claims agree.
4. Prepare evidence for all seven scoring criteria and a safe demonstration
   fallback.
5. Check sources, estimates, privacy safeguards and applicable track rules.
6. Follow the announced finalist submission and running-order instructions.
